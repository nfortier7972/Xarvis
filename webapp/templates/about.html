NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 · Planet X and SPHEREx

eXplorable Astronomical Record Viewer for Infrared Surveys

XARVIS is a public web tool for exploring NASA's SPHEREx infrared sky survey. It shows where the telescope is and what it can see, lets anyone point at a patch of sky by its coordinates, and lines that patch up with the stars and asteroids known to be there, so changes over time stand out.

The challenge

SPHEREx (Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer) launched on 12 March 2025 into a sun-synchronous orbit about 650–700 km above Earth. Over its 25-month mission it maps the entire sky in 102 infrared colours (0.75–5 µm), once every six months. Anything that moves between those maps, such as a nearby brown dwarf, an asteroid or a distant undiscovered planet, shows up as a change from one visit to the next.

The challenge asks for a public tool where users enter the coordinates of a patch of sky and watch SPHEREx's images of it play like a short video. It names five test targets: Barnard's Star, Epsilon Indi B, WISE 0855-0714, RW Aurigae and R Draconis.

What XARVIS does today

SPHEREx, liveThe telescope's real orbit around Earth, with the planets, the Moon, 12,700 real asteroids and the stars, at this moment.
What SPHEREx can seeThe sky it can point at right now (91° from the Sun and not behind Earth), and everything it can reach over an orbit.
Go to coordinatesType RA/Dec or pick a test target: the view turns to it and the patch opens for analysis. Double-click the sky does the same.
Sky point analysisCan SPHEREx see it, and when; how far the target moves during the survey (ESA Gaia); which asteroids are in the patch (NASA/JPL).
Track any objectSearch and filter asteroids by type, place, size and known/unknown, look up any comet or asteroid live, and follow it.
Travel in timePlay forward or back up to a year per second, or jump to any date, with honest notes when accuracy drops.

The checks live in tools/verify_motion.py. They also caught real bugs during development: a 30-second timing slip in SPHEREx's position, and years-old asteroid orbits that were millions of kilometres off. Both were fixed.

Design decisions

  • Simple first, detail on request. The main screen says in one sentence where SPHEREx is. Orbital elements, accuracy notes and credits sit in a collapsed Advanced section.
  • Honest about limits. When something is modelled rather than measured, or accuracy drops (far dates, fast playback), the viewer says so.
  • Smooth on ordinary computers. Lines are trimmed to the screen, asteroids are thinned when zoomed out, and resolution adapts if a computer struggles.
  • A familiar look. Square panels, small spaced capitals and agency red and blue, in the plain style of NASA, ESA, CSA and JAXA material. It is a style, not an official brand.

Known limits: star motion uses proper motion only; planets cannot be predicted beyond about ±5,000 years; SPHEREx's Moon and flight-direction avoidance angles are not published, so they are not drawn; WISE 0855-0714 is too faint for Gaia, so it is shown at the challenge's coordinates without motion.

What comes next

Every patch already packages its coordinates, time, SPHEREx's position, the target's motion and the known asteroids. The next step connects it to NASA/IPAC IRSA to fetch the SPHEREx images of that patch and play them as a time-lapse. On top of that come:

  • stacks per detector array for cleaner images;
  • spectra built on the fly from all 102 bands;
  • adding and subtracting images to reveal movers;
  • Gaia and WISE overlays;
  • shareable links with the coordinates in the URL.
